I make OTC hearing aids, so I can give you the transmit power numbers. I can't tell you what they mean for your health, and I'll say why at the end.

Bluetooth is banded by class. Class 2, which is what nearly every consumer device uses, tops out at 2.5 milliwatts. Class 1 goes to 100. Hearing aids sit at the bottom of even Class 2, and the reason isn't safety, it's the battery. A size 312 zinc air cell has high internal impedance, so a hard transmit burst sags the voltage and the aid resets. So we design the radio down until it stops doing that. On most of what I've worked on that lands under 1 milliwatt.

For scale, the phone in your pocket runs the same Bluetooth radio at the same class. Its cellular radio is a different animal and can hit one to two watts on some bands, though power control keeps the average far below the peak. That's roughly a thousand times the aid, held against your head.

There's a second thing that's specific to my side. These devices have no room for a real antenna. What you get is a folded trace, or the receiver wire doing double duty. Gain is tiny. A lot of that already weak signal gets absorbed by your own head before it goes anywhere.

Now the part I won't do. Whether any of this is harmful is a question for people who study RF and biology, not for someone who builds the things. I'd be the last person you should trust on it, since I have an obvious interest in the answer.

So take the numbers, not my conclusion. If milliwatts near your head worry you, the aid isn't where I'd start looking.
