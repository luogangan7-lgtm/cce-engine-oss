Disclosure up front: I work on the manufacturing/OEM side of OTC hearing aids. No product talk below and no links — this is just the part I actually know, and I keep seeing it come up here.



In the last few days this sub has had someone whose aids grab every bit of audio on their phone, two people trying to get aids working with a work computer, and someone who needs iPhone, Mac and PC at once. Different brands, different symptoms, same root cause: "Bluetooth" on a hearing aid spec sheet isn't one thing. There are four, they behave differently, and the sheet almost never tells you which one you're buying.



\*\*Bluetooth Classic\*\* — the same profile a wireless headset uses. Works with almost any phone regardless of OS version, and it's the only one that reliably shows up on a Windows PC as an audio device. It's also the most power-hungry, so streaming runtime is shortest. Phonak is the best-known example.



\*\*MFi (Made for iPhone)\*\* — Apple's own low-energy protocol. Works on iPhone, iPad and Mac. There is no MFi on Windows at all, so if you need a work PC, MFi alone will never get you there no matter how good it is on your phone.



\*\*ASHA\*\* — Google's low-energy streaming for Android. This is the one that catches people out. It isn't simply "Android 10 and up": it also needs a compatible Bluetooth chipset, so support varies by phone model rather than by Android version. Pixels and flagship Samsungs generally work; plenty of budget and mid-range phones don't, even on a current Android build. That's why two people with "the same Android version" get opposite results.



\*\*LE Audio (with the Hearing Access Profile)\*\* — the new standard, LC3 codec, and what Auracast is built on. Better sound at lower power, which is the first time those two have moved in the same direction. The catch is the other end: your phone or laptop needs an LE Audio-capable Bluetooth 5.3 radio, and on Windows that support is still patchy, especially on managed work machines.



Two things worth knowing that aren't faults, even though they feel like it:



\*\*Your aids "taking over" all audio on Android is by design.\*\* Under ASHA and LE Audio, Android registers hearing aids as an accessibility device and gives them priority for media routing. They reconnect aggressively and pull audio back from speakers on purpose. Nothing is broken; it's the OS doing what it was told.



\*\*Android 16 and 17 reworked the audio routing stack.\*\* A fair number of recent "it broke after an update" reports are phone-side, not device-side. Your aids didn't change.



One last thing that's purely commercial: when you're comparing battery life between models, ask whether the quoted runtime is standalone or streaming. Those are very different numbers and vendors usually quote the flattering one without saying which it is.



If you're shopping and connectivity matters, the useful questions are: which protocols does it support, which chipset provides them, is there a fallback if my phone doesn't do LE Audio, and what's the streaming runtime.



Happy to be corrected on any brand-specific detail — the fitting-side people here know plenty I don't.
