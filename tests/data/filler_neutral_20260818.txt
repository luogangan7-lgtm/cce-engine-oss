A tuning fork consists of a two-pronged metal bar joined at a base. When the bar is struck against a surface, the prongs move apart and together at a rate determined by their length, thickness, and the elastic modulus of the alloy. Longer prongs move more slowly. The base transmits comparatively little motion, which is why a fork can be held there without damping the prongs. Placing the base against a hollow wooden box couples the motion to a larger surface area and increases the volume of the emitted tone without changing its rate. Standard laboratory forks are milled from steel or aluminium and are stamped with a number indicating the rate of motion. Temperature shifts that number slightly, because the elastic modulus of the alloy changes with heat; a fork calibrated at twenty degrees will read a fraction lower when warmed. For this reason calibration certificates state a reference temperature. Forks are manufactured in sets covering a range of values, and the intervals between members of a set are fixed at manufacture rather than adjusted afterwards. A fork that has been dropped may develop a hairline crack at the junction of prong and base, which shortens the duration of the tone and lowers the rate; such forks are withdrawn from calibrated sets.
